We have already pointed out that COVID-19 case data is affected by testing capacity, reporting rules, and the level of government transparency, while GDP growth is only a high-level macroeconomic indicator. If users only see a superficial relationship like "high cases - low GDP," they can easily take it as the whole truth, ignoring underreporting, informal economic activity, and group differences. In other words, this problem space is not just a data presentation issue, but an ethical issue of honestly presenting uncertainty and avoiding misinterpretations.
The second core human experience is the balance between privacy and the public interest. Your report explicitly limits its analysis to the national and regional levels, excluding detailed hospital records, company-level financial data, or any sensitive personal information, because such content poses significant privacy and ethical risks and is unsuitable for a public-facing design. In other words, your problem space itself contains a moral judgment: to help the public understand the relationship between the pandemic and the economy, securely shareable aggregated data should be used, rather than pursuing more granular data at the expense of individual privacy.

Testing showed that participants were confident in their misinterpretations. If users believed they understood the meaning of "Recovery vs 2020," then they had no reason to consult the glossary. They required users to question their current understanding and choose to explore. Our participants, however, did not question; they remained confident. A mechanism that requires user uncertainty to trigger cannot interrupt a user's confident misinterpretation.
The third solution we ultimately decided on was the "Indicator Guide Card": an automatically triggered, context-sensitive explanation that appears when a user first encounters each indicator in the onboarding process, before they have a chance to read and interpret the charts. The guide card appears upon first contact, rather than on demand.
Our designed guide card contains four elements, each targeting a specific link in the chain of failures we observed:
A color-coded domain badge: Located before the indicator name, it is prominently labeled "Economy" or "Health." This identifies the domain before the user reads the label, thus addressing the first step in the failure chain.
A plain-understand definition: Avoiding the use of secondary technical terms introduced in our notes (e.g., instead of "bounced back from the trough," use "how much has the economy recovered since bottoming out in 2020").
A practical numerical example: Using real data from our dataset (e.g., "The UK economy bottomed out in Q2 2020 and then grew by 8.9 percent in 2023"), the metrics and units of measurement are built on a concrete, easily understood case before the charts are presented.

Describe a point in your design process where your team had multiple possible directions

1. The decision point

The clearest fork in our project came after Week 8, when we had built four competing prototypes for the same research question. The question was not which chart looks best, but which representation helps non-expert students interpret COVID-economy relationships with traceable evidence.

Direction P1: 2D dashboard. Apparent strength: fast reading, easy to quote numbers.

Direction P2: Immersive narrative. Apparent strength: emotional engagement, cinematic context.

Direction P3: Policy simulator. Apparent strength: what-if causality framing through decisions.

Direction P4: 3D COVID Economic Globe. Apparent strength: spatial exploration, strong visual linkage.

At the same time, tutor feedback on A01 had flagged that we were still framing a visualization problem rather than a user interpretation problem. Week 6 testing on the globe had already suggested we were collecting interaction behaviour (rotation, clicking) more than interpretation evidence (what GDP vs 2019 means, correlation vs causation).

So we did some key decisions:

Path A: Continue globe-first (P4). Lean into the format users said they preferred. Keep investing in 3D navigation, timeline, and linked panels.

Path B: Pivot to interpretation-first guided comparison. Retire the globe as the primary research instrument. Merge dashboard comparison (Option A) with staged narrative scaffolding (Option B).

Path C: Split the team and keep polishing all four formats for showcase breadth. We rejected this because it would dilute validation and leave research question 2 unanswered.

We chose Path B, documented as Guided Comparison Dashboard plus Narrative Evidence Flow, and not continuing as a globe-first interface.

2. Evidence that forced the choice

2.1 Week 6 process evidence

We retrospectively found that open tasks such as explore the globe produced thin description. Participants spent time on navigation. By the time we asked about economic meaning, the session was almost over. Mixed exploratory and evaluative tasks in one slot made it hard to tell whether confusion came from layout, wording, or lack of economics background. That aligned with Gap A in our Rumsfeld matrix: users can operate the interface but may not interpret health-economy linkage.

2.2 Week 8 comparative questionnaire

All four respondents tried every prototype. Ratings were not uniform.

Several participants rated P4 (globe) highest on clarity, usability, and COVID-economy linkage. For example, Respondent 1 gave all 5s on P4 for favorite and best for relationship.

P3 (simulator) sometimes scored high on linkage but low on usability, suggesting the game frame helped see connection without making comparison easy.

Critical divergence: Respondent 3 preferred P1 (dashboard) as favorite prototype but still named P4 as best for explaining the COVID-economy relationship. That is Gap C (preference does not equal comprehension) showing up in real data.

Open comments reinforced the split. Reasons for liking P4 included more intuitive, faster to see key data, better shows health-economy link, and more attractive visually. That is a mix of comprehension claims and aesthetic appeal, which we could not treat as proof of understanding without follow-up interpretation tasks.

2.3 Literature and problem reframing

Our literature list and claim-evidence table supported that non-experts misread time-series and conflate metrics; format choice affects cognitive load; trust is multidimensional and design-sensitive; and guided or narrative structure can improve student economic comprehension.

Together with tutor feedback, this justified scaffolding and traceability as design requirements, not more 3D novelty.

2.4 Assumptions we had to test

Our gap matrix listed assumptions such as more interactive equals better understanding and more metrics equals higher confidence. Week 8 did not validate those. It only showed format preference variability. Choosing globe-first would have doubled down on an unvalidated assumption.

3. The design decision and reasoning

Decision: Stop treating the 3D globe as the primary research artifact. Build a merged guided comparison and narrative flow prototype aimed at primary users (policy, design, and economics-adjacent students who must justify claims in coursework).

Reasoning:

First, problem-first. Tutor critique required moving from we have data so we visualize it to students lack scaffolding to interpret linked indicators, compare across countries and time, and separate evidence from causation.

Second, match format to failure mode. The globe excelled at engagement and spatial overview but Week 6 showed weak verbal explanation and metric semantics. The dashboard excelled at side-by-side values and citation. Narrative flow excelled at sequencing health, economy, and boundary, and collecting confidence and trust.

Third, research design. We needed instruments that separate liked from understood. A globe-first UI makes preference salient early. Our later protocol collects preference last.

Fourth, scope control. Course timeline and tutor expectation favoured one coherent hi-fi direction for showcase, not four half-validated products.

Concrete structure after the decision:

Version 1: Four screens: Metric Primer, Guided UK and Australia comparison, Interpretation Builder, Trust and Source.

validation: Primer as a gated separate screen failed. Compliance theatre when users skipped primer quickly. Health misreads persisted. Sequential flow caused friction. One participant said four screens are a lot.

Second fork within Path B: four-screen sequential versus single progressive canvas with five layers. We chose progressive canvas. Hi-fi confirmed fewer flow complaints and better domain anchoring via persistent ECONOMIC and HEALTH badges.

So the one path was a committed line: interpretation-first scaffolding, iteratively tightening how guidance is delivered from sequential screens to embedded progressive layers.

4. Was this the right direction?

What went right:

Separate screens and primer-as-gate was the wrong mechanism. This validation showed primer skippable, misreads when skipped, and friction on screen switching.

Ambient domain labels and enforced citation worked better. Second validation misreads fell from 3 out of 10 to 0 out of 6; 6 out of 6 interpretation submissions contained numerical values; 5 out of 6 used non-causal language in follow-up.

Trust must be gated on source engagement. Second validation: all participants entered limitation text before trust; none gave high trust without source engagement.

Preference versus understanding remains real. Survey Respondent 3: favorite P1, best for relationship P4.

What remains uncertain:

We may have under-used the globe strengths. P4 still won many preference and linkage scores. We did not fully test a hybrid of globe for overview plus guided panel for interpretation. Path B was a bet on measurability for coursework users, not proof that globe is worse for all tasks.

Aesthetic and trust confound. Some open comments tied liking P4 to visual appeal. Without interpretation tasks tied to each format, we cannot claim Path B would have scored higher on comprehension in Week 8, only that later cycles improved interpretation metrics on the new instrument.

Generalisability. Participants were mostly course peers. Argument cultures may differ across DECO, ECON, and POLS.

Overall judgment:

Choosing interpretation-first guided comparison over globe-first was the right direction given our evidence and constraints at that moment. It aligned with tutor feedback, literature on non-expert misreading, our Week 6 failure modes, and emerging preference-comprehension divergence in Week 8.

It was not right in the sense of permanently retiring spatial exploration. The globe remains useful as an exploratory layer. It was right as the primary validation vehicle for our research questions.

The subsequent shift from four-screen flow to progressive canvas was further supported by the first validation and the second validation and strengthened Path B rather than contradicting it.

If we had stayed on globe-first, we would likely have optimised visual details that respondents mentioned while under-testing metric semantics, causation boundaries, and trust calibration, which our tutor and literature said mattered most.

Negative effect: False confidence, weaker assignments, or embarrassment in classroom settings.

4. Economics-literate users who find guided flows constraining (negatively affected by friction)

second test participants with economics background complained about too many screens and forced primer (for example comments wanting one page instead of four screens). Forced sequencing over-managed users who already understood metrics.

Negative effect: Friction, feeling talked down to, abandoning before completion. third test reduced screen-switch complaints but added layers and validation that some still found constraining (S01 wanted edit-after-boundary, addressed later).

These users are not excluded but may perform worse on preference scores even when comprehension is high.

5. Users with visual, cognitive, or motor access needs (potentially excluded)

our design relies on colour-coded ECONOMIC and HEALTH badges, expandable chips, progressive disclosure, mouse or keyboard interaction, and dense comparison panels. Fixed max width around 1400px appears in our specs.

Who may be excluded or harmed:

Users with colour vision deficiency if badges are colour-only without redundant text (we have text labels, which helps, but contrast and non-colour cues should be verified).

Users who use screen readers: complex layered canvas and dynamic reveal may be poorly announced if not built to WCAG.

Users with vestibular or spatial sensitivity: our earlier globe prototype triggered survey comments about discomfort with 3D balls, dense fear, nausea. If globe remains as optional view, harm could recur.

Users with cognitive load limits: five layers plus writing tasks may overwhelm more than a simple chart.

Negative effect: Cannot access content equally, or physical discomfort, which is an equity issue in classroom deployment.

6. English language and academic genre requirements (exclusion)

Interview scripts, interpretation templates, and boundary tasks are in academic English. International students, students with ESL backgrounds, or community users with lower English proficiency may be excluded even if they understand economics in another language.

Negative effect: Appears as low comprehension in testing when the barrier is language, biasing our research conclusions toward native or fluent English speakers.

7. Users whose countries or communities are absent or misrepresented in the data story (negative societal effect)

our showcase comparison centres UK and Australia. Survey and course samples are small and peer-based.

Why negatively affected:

Countries with weak reporting, different testing regimes, or political manipulation of statistics may look worse or better than reality when only national aggregates are shown. our ethics writing notes misrepresentation risk when cases and GDP are shown without context, as in Argentina reporting examples.

National GDP and recovery averages hide sectoral and labour-market inequality (tourism, low-wage workers invisible). Users may conclude a country recovered on paper while local harm continues.

Negative effect: Stigmatisation of certain regions, invisible suffering, policy narratives that oversimplify winners and losers.

8. People who distrust official statistics (trust harm in both directions)

Some audiences do not believe government or World Bank data because of political bias or under-reporting.

Negative effects:

If the tool looks credible and professional, users like second test S03 may over-trust branded sources without engaging limitations.

If users already distrust data, mandatory source panels may feel cynical or insufficient, and the tool may amplify scepticism without offering participatory or local data.

Trust gate improves engagement in third test but does not solve political mistrust of underlying datasets.

9. Casual or exploratory users (excluded from intended interaction model)

Users who want fast emotional overview (immersive prototype appeal) or game-like exploration (simulator) may dislike mandatory interpretation, numerical validation, and limitation writing.

Survey respondents liked globe for visual appeal and speed. our chosen path prioritises interpretation over exploration.

Negative effect: Disengagement, feeling the tool is homework not discovery.

10. Small-sample and discipline bias in our validation (systemic exclusion)

Testing with n equals 4 survey and n equals 6 to 8 qualitative sessions, mostly university peers in Australian course context, means findings may not transfer to other ages, cultures, or education levels.

Negative effect: Design optimised for a narrow cohort is presented as general solution, excluding voices not in the room.

11. Users on low-end devices or slow networks (technical exclusion)

our Week 5 ethics note: heavy dashboards shut out slow phones and less experienced users. WebGL globe and multi-panel web apps increase load.

Negative effect: Cannot run prototype, slower interaction, higher abandonment among already marginalised users.

12. Users harmed by false causal narratives even when warned

Passive or active boundary tasks reduce but do not eliminate causal language. second test showed correct multiple choice with causal reversion in open answers. Media priors can override interface framing.

Negative effect: Users leave with a story that COVID caused GDP drops because trends align, which can fuel blame narratives against countries or groups.

PART 3: HOW our TEAM COULD ADDRESS OR MITIGATE THESE ISSUES

Mitigations already in our design (document these as intentional equity measures)

Separate preference from comprehension in study protocol; collect preference last to avoid rewarding only polish.

Persistent domain badges and on-demand definition chips to reduce health versus economy conflation.

Constructed boundary self-check with revision instead of recognition-only multiple choice.

Trust score gated on limitation text plus scaffold prompt for substantive engagement.

Explicit scope: no epidemiological forecasting, no automated policy prescription, no causal claims beyond data.

Correlation and limitation language in interpretation template.

Progressive canvas instead of skippable primer screen to reduce compliance theatre.

Edit interpretation link after boundary check for users who need non-linear paths.

Mitigations we should add or plan (recommended for report and future iteration)

A. Access and inclusion

Run accessibility audit: colour contrast, keyboard navigation, screen reader labels for layers, focus order, option to reduce motion.

Provide non-colour encodings for ECONOMIC and HEALTH (icons, patterns, text always visible).

Offer simplified mode: comparison plus definitions only, skip extended writing for low-stakes exploration.

Test with ESL participants or provide plain-language and glossary in additional languages if deployed internationally.

Support responsive lawet and lighter asset bundle for low-bandwidth devices; avoid globe as only entry.

B. Literacy and cognitive load

Adaptive paths: skip or shorten primer layers when user demonstrates correct paraphrase in a check question.

Replace minimum character count with quality rubric or example-based prompt (we already rejected 50-char padding for good reason).

Optional audio walkthrough or short video for percentage points and baselines.

Tutor mode or instructor dashboard to see where students stuck without grading only final text.

C. Data ethics and representation

Expand beyond two countries; let users select regions relevant to them; show reporting quality flags on case data.

Add sectoral or regional breakdown where data allows, or explicit banner when only national aggregates exist.

Surface inequality note: national GDP does not show distribution across industries or workers.

Link to methodology and data vintage prominently; distinguish snapshot date from live API.

Co-design sessions with users from underrepresented backgrounds, not only DECO peers.

D. Trust and misuse

Do not allow trust scores without limitation engagement (already done); strengthen scaffold for quality limitations.

Add clear banner: for coursework and learning, not for medical or policy decisions.

Train facilitators not to correct during tasks but debrief misconceptions afterward.

Document known unknowns in UI so users cannot confuse interface polish with truth.

E. Research and fairness in evaluation

Increase sample size and recruit across disciplines and familiarity levels.

Report when benefits concentrate on medium-familiarity users and when very low familiarity users remain overconfident.

Analyse harms separately: who completed flow but still used causal or health-recovery language.

F. Expert and casual users

Optional expert view: raw table export, no forced template.

Optional explore mode without gates for first contact, then guided mode for assignment tasks.

PART 4: BALANCED CONCLUSION FOR our ASSIGNMENT

our system is most likely to benefit non-expert university students who must learn to justify COVID-economy comparisons with cited numbers and explicit limits on what data can prove. It can indirectly help informed public audiences if simplified and broadened beyond a two-country teaching comparison.

It may disadvantage or exclude users with very low data literacy, high expertise, significant accessibility needs, limited English, low-end technology, or lived experience misaligned with national aggregates. It may negatively affect broader society if misread comparisons fuel stigma, hide inequality, or increase misplaced trust in polished visuals.

our team has already mitigated several cognitive and trust harms through scaffolding, badges, boundary construction, and source gating. The largest remaining gaps are representational fairness (whose countries and workers appear), accessibility and language equity, and preventing overconfidence among users who complete the flow without internalising boundaries. Addressing those requires design changes plus more diverse participant research, not only interface polish.
