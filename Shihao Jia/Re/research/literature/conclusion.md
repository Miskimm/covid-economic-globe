## English translation

## Conclusions that can be drawn (with literature support)

1. The relationship between the pandemic and the economy is real, complex, and worth examining and comparing

Category 1 (Refs 1 Chetty, 2 multi-country CGE, 3 OECD) shows that economic shocks and recoveries differ across countries and economic structures differ. Placing health shocks and economic outcomes in the same analytical frame is meaningful. Cross-country and cross-time comparison is not “visualization for its own sake.”

→ This answers: the relationship is worth looking at, and the data scope is justified.

2. There are “non-expert” users who struggle to understand data and need support—not just more raw numbers

Category 2 (Refs 4–7) shows that even educated people misread pandemic time series, numbers, and charts; information sources affect misunderstanding; non-experts need scaffolded interpretation, not data dumps alone.

Category 4 (Refs 11–13) shows that non-experts face high cognitive load with economic data and that current tools lack analytical interaction for novices (Ref 12 explicitly describes an interpretation gap). Visualization plus guided text helps economic understanding more than text alone.

→ This answers: some people need to “make sense of” data, and current presentations often fall short.

3. There is a clear user group: university students who must interpret and argue about economic (and complex) data in coursework

Category 5 (Refs 14–16) shows that students in economics-related courses must build arguments through visual, narrative, and interactive tasks. Interactive visualization helps them approach problems more like experts.

→ This answers: students have coursework tasks that require understanding and expressing economic meaning. This aligns with your primary user definition.

4. Taken together, the three points above support the claim that users need to view/understand the pandemic–economy relationship—more precisely:

Users need to understand what health and economic indicators mean in the same context, compare across countries, and avoid misreading and over-inference. The issue is not “whether anyone cares about COVID,” but “whether people must link the two types of information for a task, while current presentations often lead to misreading or surface-level trust.”

Strongest combined wording example:

- Relationship exists, task is meaningful: Refs 1, 2, 3
- Users misread data, need interpretation support: Refs 4, 6, 12
- Specific user group (students) has argumentation needs: Refs 14, 16

## What the literature cannot prove on its own (boundaries—important for assignments)

Claim that literature alone cannot establish Reason
“Everyone needs to see pandemic–economy linkage” Most sources discuss misunderstanding, cognitive load, students/public—not a population-wide needs survey
“Users explicitly want a health+economy tool on one screen” Ref 12 addresses economic visualization gaps; Refs 4/6 focus more on pandemic or numeracy. Few list entries specifically on “users need to see both together.” Your testing (first/second/third test) must fill this gap
“A 3D globe is the necessary format” The list barely addresses interface form; Ref 7 only shows that format affects understanding
“Viewing the tool ensures correct causal judgment” Category 1 does not prove causation; Category 2 shows people tend to over-infer

## One-sentence answer to the question

Yes—you can argue that “there are users who need to view and understand the relationship between COVID-19 and the economy.” Literature supports this along three lines: the relationship is worth analyzing; users misread data; students/non-experts need support to interpret and argue.

A more rigorous wording: users need to link pandemic-related and economic indicators in task contexts and justify their claims, but current presentations often cause misreading, overload, or misplaced trust. Design should offer interpretation support, source transparency, and boundary cues—not another panel that only displays numbers.
